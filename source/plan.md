# A Student's Guide to Using AI — the plan

Working plan for the guide. Supersedes outline.md. Everything here is agreed with Mark unless marked open.

## What it is

A free, fully accessible PDF guide for secondary students (11-19), pitched at a literate Year 9 reader. It teaches what AI is and how it fails, then how to make informed choices about using it, through Mark's digital cognition framework. Microsite to follow at aiforstudents.ictevangelist.com. Free download from ictevangelist.com.

## Voice

Mark's voice as he'd speak to Year 9: direct questions to the reader, plain English, contractions, technical terms used and explained rather than avoided, references inside teen experience (predictive text, group chats, revision). Never talks down. No AI mannerisms, no corporate gloss.

## Design

- Portrait A4. Roughly 6-10 pages per chapter plus full-page infographics rotated 90 degrees (landscape on the page).
- Fresh identity, faithful to the ICT Evangelist brand but distinct. Ribbon rainbow kept from the Pedagogy First guide.
- Fat modern display face for headings, light accessible face for body (Lexend the leading candidate).
- One icon per chapter, used consistently. Dual coding throughout: diagrams that carry the idea, not decoration.
- Accessibility is a first-class requirement: tagged PDF, true reading order, alt text on every image and icon, contrast-checked palette, generous type sizes and spacing.
- Every chapter closes with a "3 things" panel: three takeaways, three things to try today, or three top tips, whichever fits the chapter.

## The six chapters

### 1. What AI actually is
The mental model the rest of the guide stands on. Opens with the database misconception, corrects it with prediction, anchored in predictive text. LLM unpacked through its own name (large, language, model). Probable is not the same as true. It learned from human writing, which sets up chapters 2 and 3.
Infographic: the prediction loop (a sentence mid-generation with probability bars).
Status: draft 2 written and in review (chapters/01-what-ai-actually-is.md).

### 2. When it gets things wrong
Hallucinations. Why a probability machine produces confident falsehoods, why fluency reads as authority, invented sources and fake citations. How to check: verification habits, triangulating against sources you trust, asking where a claim comes from. Ends with the checking habits that make AI safe to use for school work.
Infographic: anatomy of a hallucination (a plausible-looking answer dissected, showing what was real and what was invented).
Integrity tie-in: retaining and checking outputs, first mention of acknowledging AI use.

### 3. Bias
Built on Mark's Types of Bias in AI Outputs infographic (bias-types.md): five categories, 21 types, recreated natively in the guide's design language as a full spread. The chapter's key insight: bias is easy to see in image outputs and much harder to see in text. A generated image shows you its assumptions instantly; generated text carries the same assumptions invisibly.
Infographic: the bias types spread (full page, rotated), plus a paired image-vs-text example showing the same bias visible and invisible.

### 4. Digital cognition: the five Cs
Confidence, competence, cognisance, consistency, context (five-cs.md). Credited to Mark and Olly Lewis, The EdTech Playbook. Cognisance carries the chapter: the informed choice to use or not use AI, because sometimes not using it is the better choice. Student-facing definitions needed (open: Mark writes them, or drafts to Mark for edit).
Infographic: the five Cs diagram recreated for students (four Cs inside the circle, context as the outer ring).

### 5. AI in your subjects
Context and consistency in practice across eight subjects: English, Maths, Science, History, Geography, Languages, Arts, Technology. For each: where AI genuinely supports the learning, where it takes the learning away from you, and one worked example of good use. Honest about the difference between using AI to understand and using it to avoid understanding.
Infographic: a subject-by-subject map (full page, rotated), possibly a helps/harms grid.

### 6. Using it well, using it fairly
The universal approaches that work in any subject, drawing on Ethan Mollick with plain credit and fresh expositions (verify his published approaches at source before drafting). Mark's STAIR prompting approach (stair.md) recreated with student-facing example prompts written in the same shape as his teacher examples. Then the rules and the boundaries: the JCQ requirements for AI use in assessed work, quoted (verify final wording against the live JCQ document before publication; jcq.org.uk blocks automated access, so Mark checks the master PDF), and what you should never share with a chatbot. Closes the guide with the personal standard: your work, your judgement, your name on it.
Infographics: STAIR recreated; a "never share" panel; the JCQ rules as a clear reference card.

## Rough size

Six chapters at 6-10 pages plus front and back matter lands the guide around 45-65 pages. Tighten in design.

## Tool references

Tool-agnostic throughout: "the LLM tool of choice in your school". No named products in examples.
